Industry, hospital and community split foundation training: what to expect

Newly allocated an industry, hospital and community split? This source-backed guide explains likely day-to-day work, how industry teams differ, realistic project examples, preparation for GPhC learning outcomes and what can help you move into an industry career after registration.

By Phivos Michailides · Independent guidance · Reviewed 21 November 2026 · 16 detailed questions

IN-DEPTH GUIDE · INDUSTRY CAREERS

Industry, hospital & community: your split placement explained

16 practical, source-backed answers about the rotation, real industry teams, projects, the foundation year and what comes next.

An industry / hospital / community split can provide a valuable introduction to medicines development without sacrificing the foundation year's clinical purpose. But the quality of the opportunity depends on the actual programme, not the words “industry placement”. This guide answers the questions newly allocated trainees most often ask, drawing on NHS England training guidance, ABPI careers material, MHRA safety guidance, employer examples and a published cross-sector study.

The examples of tasks and timetables are illustrative. Placement Atlas does not have private access to individual employers' rotas or guaranteed projects. Confirm current details with your host and the official training provider.

01What does an industry / hospital / community split actually mean?

It means your foundation training is delivered through more than one setting, with an industry organisation as one of the hosts. The clinical settings and industry setting should contribute to a coherent, supervised foundation training plan. It does not mean three equal four-month rotations. A programme might have different blocks, an order chosen by the employer, or a principal employment site and one or more rotation partners.

For your own allocation, ask the employer for a written outline of the weeks in each setting, their sequence, all exact addresses, working patterns and the name of the lead designated supervisor. NHS England explains that employers operate approved training programmes and that variations exist between programmes; its 2026 employer requirements describe specific supervision arrangements for longer multi-sector rotations. Treat the individual Oriel advert and confirmed employer training plan as authoritative, not a generic three-sector description.

The industry period may focus on regulatory affairs, medical information, drug safety, manufacturing, research, quality or another business unit. Some posts are explicitly attached to a department. Others include introductions to several teams. Ask which experiences are planned and what is only possible if capacity permits.

Sources and further reading: NHS England — foundation training guidance · NHS England — 2026 employer training requirements · NHS England — preferencing and programme information

02What is a realistic day in the industry rotation?

Many office-based placements combine induction, reading scientific or procedural material, team meetings, working on a supervised task, reviewing outputs and short feedback discussions. Depending on the host, you might be summarising clinical evidence, checking a document against an approved source, helping prepare a presentation, observing a cross-functional meeting, or learning how a medicine's safety information is tracked.

An illustrative day might begin by checking a project plan and reading a paper or standard operating procedure; continue with a medical-information, regulatory or safety team meeting; include a focused document or data task; and end with supervisor feedback and portfolio reflection. This is a plausible example, not a timetable promised by an employer. Some rotations are laboratory-, manufacturing- or site-based and look very different from desk-based medical affairs.

The pace can feel more project-based than routine patient-facing pharmacy: deadlines, version-controlled documents, scientific accuracy, confidentiality and communication matter. Trainees should not expect independent authority to approve regulated work or to give external scientific advice without appropriate oversight. Reading a previous trainee's first-person account can help you formulate questions, but historical examples are not a description of a current programme.

Sources and further reading: ABPI — pharmaceutical industry careers · ABPI — medical information · Pharmaceutical Journal — historical trainee day-in-the-life

03What projects might a trainee be asked to work on?

Examples to ask about rather than expect: an evidence table comparing published clinical studies; a supervised medical-information response draft; a summary of relevant product information; a regulatory document tracker; a mock adverse-event intake workflow; an approved literature-monitoring exercise; a trial-operations spreadsheet or process map; a quality-improvement proposal; or a short presentation explaining a disease area and treatment landscape.

The appropriate scope depends on the function. In medical information, a trainee might practise turning a clinical question into an accurate, referenced answer. In regulatory affairs, the exercise may involve document control, labelling changes or understanding a submission timeline. In pharmacovigilance, it could focus on recognising reportable safety information, understanding case processing and following escalation procedures. In clinical operations, it may be a trial visit checklist or study-status summary. Manufacturing and quality teams may instead focus on deviations, validation concepts and Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP).

A small, finished project with clear supervision often provides stronger experience than observing many teams without producing anything. Agree on a defined scope, evidence of completion and confidentiality rules early. Never take patient data, unpublished company information or internal slides away for a portfolio or job application without explicit permission.

Sources and further reading: ABPI — medical information · ABPI — drug safety and pharmacovigilance · ABPI — regulatory affairs · ABPI — clinical research work · ABPI — pharmacists in manufacturing

04Will I rotate through medical affairs, regulatory affairs, pharmacovigilance and clinical trials?

Not necessarily. The placement may sit in a single team. Being employed or hosted by a major pharmaceutical company does not automatically grant a full rotation across its functions. Departments have different obligations, staffing, access permissions and commercially sensitive projects. A broader overview may come through structured talks, shadowing, approved meetings and short informational interviews rather than several formal rotations.

Ask your host: “Which business unit am I assigned to? What is the main deliverable? Are there scheduled introductions to other functions? Could I arrange two or three short shadowing conversations while still completing the approved learning plan?” These questions are more informative than asking whether you will “see industry” in general.

Keep a note of the parts you enjoy: evaluating published evidence, preparing written responses, structured compliance work, data, project coordination, relationship-building or manufacturing science. That interest profile helps you choose future roles. The wide range of possible departments is supported by ABPI career descriptions, but the exact exposure is controlled by your employer.

Sources and further reading: ABPI — pharmaceutical industry careers · ABPI — medical affairs · University of Edinburgh — life sciences careers and entry routes

05What do medical affairs and medical information actually do?

Medical information supports accurate, balanced and traceable responses to questions about medicines from healthcare professionals and other appropriate audiences. The work can involve evaluating sources, preparing referenced answers, maintaining approved information, escalating safety information and supporting compliant communications. A pharmacy background can be particularly relevant because it develops medicines knowledge and skills in finding and appraising clinical evidence.

Medical affairs is broader: scientific strategy, evidence communication, medical education, external expert engagement and collaboration with clinical development, safety and other teams. Titles and responsibilities differ between companies. A trainee might observe an approved scientific meeting, review literature or learn how materials are medically reviewed; they should not assume they will independently act as a medical science liaison or sign off external materials.

A good learning objective is to compare an evidence-based, non-promotional medical answer with promotional material and understand why audience, claims, approvals and the ABPI Code matter. Ask how the medical team interacts with compliance, pharmacovigilance, regulatory and commercial colleagues.

Sources and further reading: ABPI — medical information · ABPI — medical affairs · ABPI — Code of Practice

06What would I learn in regulatory affairs?

Regulatory affairs helps make sure a medicine is developed, authorised and maintained in accordance with the relevant rules. Teams coordinate scientific and quality evidence, submissions, marketing authorisations, changes to product information, labelling and communication with regulators. Work often combines technical reading, precise writing, version control, timelines and cross-team coordination.

A trainee could learn the difference between an initial marketing authorisation, a post-authorisation variation and an update to a product information document; map the groups that contribute to a submission; or practise finding and comparing authorised information. Those are learning examples, not claims that every industry trainee has access to confidential dossiers or live regulatory submissions.

For career exploration, ask a regulatory colleague which introductory roles exist locally, what evidence of good documentation skills they look for and which qualifications are useful versus optional. ABPI notes that entrants can come from life-sciences degrees and may move into regulatory through other industry roles; you do not automatically need a PhD.

Sources and further reading: ABPI — regulatory affairs · ABPI — pharmacy graduate entering regulatory affairs

07What is pharmacovigilance, and could a trainee work on it?

Pharmacovigilance (PV) is about detecting, assessing, understanding and preventing problems associated with medicines during development and after launch. Teams may receive reports from healthcare professionals and patients, process individual case safety information, monitor literature, contribute to safety summaries and support risk-management activities.

An introductory placement may cover how suspected adverse drug reactions are identified, documented and promptly escalated through approved channels. The UK's MHRA Yellow Card scheme is a useful public example of how suspected medicine harms are reported. A trainee should learn the local reporting process before handling any real safety information: safety obligations and deadlines are not optional, and handling reports should remain supervised.

Ask the PV team what their typical workflow looks like, which data quality checks matter and how they communicate potential signals to regulatory and medical teams. Some companies combine pharmacovigilance and medical information responsibilities, while others keep them separate.

Sources and further reading: ABPI — drug safety and pharmacovigilance · MHRA — Yellow Card reporting

08What would exposure to clinical trials involve?

Clinical research teams coordinate the people, records and processes that allow trials to run safely and produce interpretable data. Depending on the organisation, introductory exposure might cover protocol reading, eligibility criteria, study timelines, document quality, site coordination, trial supplies or an explanation of informed consent and Good Clinical Practice (GCP).

Roles such as clinical trials administrator (CTA) and clinical research associate (CRA) have different responsibilities. For example, ABPI describes activities for experienced CRAs ranging from coordinating approvals to monitoring sites. A foundation trainee would normally observe or assist within an appropriate supervised scope, not immediately become an independent CRA or clinical investigator.

Useful questions include: “Does the host run its own trials or work with contract research organisations? May I attend a study-team meeting? Is introductory GCP training offered? Can I map how a protocol amendment reaches a clinical site?” Learn the research lifecycle first; then decide whether data, operations, monitoring or clinical science attracts you most.

Sources and further reading: ABPI — clinical research work · ABPI — pharmaceutical industry careers

09What about manufacturing, quality, data, market access and commercial roles?

Industry is much wider than medical affairs, regulatory affairs, pharmacovigilance and trials. Manufacturing and quality assurance teams work with production processes, batch records, deviations, investigations and GMP principles. Supply-chain teams manage availability and movement of medicines. Health economics and market access work with evidence about value, reimbursement and adoption. Data and analytics teams may support commercial, clinical or operational decisions. Project-management and business roles keep multidisciplinary work on schedule.

These functions can appeal to pharmacists who enjoy systems, technology, writing, data, structured processes or the broader medicine lifecycle. They also explain why the company hosting a placement is not a reliable shortcut to guessing the work: a regulatory placement at one site can differ fundamentally from a manufacturing placement elsewhere.

If you are unsure which route to pursue, request informational conversations with colleagues in two or three contrasting functions. Ask what they actually do, what they wish they had learned early and which entry-level roles recruit MPharm graduates. Keep the distinction between commercial strategy, scientific medical information and approved promotional activity clear.

Sources and further reading: ABPI — pharmacists in manufacturing · University of Edinburgh — life sciences careers and entry routes · ABPI — pharmaceutical industry careers · ABPI — Code of Practice

10How do the hospital and community rotations help someone aiming for industry?

Hospital pharmacy can develop clinical reasoning, medicines reconciliation, multidisciplinary communication, drug-information skills and an understanding of specialist care pathways. Community pharmacy can build practical knowledge of dispensing systems, medicines safety, consultations, adherence barriers, patient access and real-world medicine use. Those experiences can make industry work more meaningful: you better understand the healthcare settings where a medicine will eventually be used.

For example, a patient question about side effects can connect community experience to pharmacovigilance; a hospital formulary discussion can illuminate evidence generation and market access; a medicines-information enquiry can connect to medical affairs. These are examples of transferable insight, not permission to collect patient information for industry projects.

The clinical rotations are also part of your actual foundation training, not an inconvenient detour from an industry career. NHS England's assessment and e-portfolio approach and the employer's training plan remain central. Check how your designated supervisor and rotational supervisors coordinate feedback, GPhC learning outcomes and preparation for the registration assessment.

Sources and further reading: NHS England — foundation training guidance · NHS England — 2026 employer training requirements · University Hospitals Birmingham — example foundation programme

11What should I learn before the industry placement starts?

Start with the medicine lifecycle: discovery → preclinical research → clinical development → regulatory approval → manufacturing and supply → monitoring after launch. Learn the basic vocabulary of trials (protocol, endpoint, randomisation, adverse event), medicine information (SmPC, evidence appraisal), regulatory affairs (marketing authorisation, variation), drug safety (adverse drug reaction, signal) and quality (GMP). This foundation helps you understand conversations without pretending to be a specialist.

Practise finding reliable sources and summarising a short clinical paper: research question, design, population, outcome, limitations and practical meaning. Refresh Excel tables, basic charts, PowerPoint and professional email writing, since these may be useful across many office-based teams. If the host offers specific training in GCP, information governance or its internal systems, follow its materials rather than paying for generic courses first.

Before day one, ask which department you are joining, whether there are suggested papers or onboarding modules, the expected dress and attendance arrangements, whether equipment is provided, and how work will be mapped to the foundation portfolio. Doing a little targeted reading beats trying to master every pharma function.

Sources and further reading: ABPI — pharmaceutical industry careers · ABPI — clinical research work · ABPI — Code of Practice

12How can I make the most of a short industry rotation?

Before arrival: request your schedule and supervisor details; set two or three learning goals. Examples: “Understand the medical-information workflow,” “Complete one accurately referenced project,” or “Meet colleagues in two other functions.”

First weeks: learn the safety, confidentiality and document-control rules; ask your supervisor what successful performance looks like; understand where your work fits into a broader medicine process. Keep a private learning log that records your skills and reflections, not company confidential information or patient details.

Middle of the rotation: volunteer for an appropriately scoped project, request feedback early, and ask for permitted shadowing. Record what changed following your feedback: a clearer evidence table, better communication with colleagues, a faster checked workflow or a stronger presentation.

Final weeks: present your approved learning to the team, ask for a development conversation and identify roles worth researching. Request LinkedIn connections or future career advice professionally, without assuming a job will be available. Make sure any evidence included in your GPhC portfolio or personal CV complies with your employer's policy.

Sources and further reading: NHS England — foundation training guidance · NHS England — 2026 employer training requirements · ABPI — real job case studies

13Can this help me get an industry job after registration?

Yes, it can be useful evidence of interest, understanding and professional contacts; no, it is not a guaranteed job offer. A graduate with an industry split may be able to explain what a specific team does, how it handles evidence and compliance, and what they contributed. That is often much stronger than a generic statement that “I have always been interested in pharma”. But a hospital-only or community-only training year does not close off industry as a career.

Routes include graduate programmes, entry-level positions and function-specific assistant or associate roles. Depending on the employer, search for medical information associate, regulatory affairs associate, drug safety associate, clinical trials administrator, clinical operations coordinator, quality associate, medical writer or graduate schemes. Some roles require additional expertise or experience, so read person specifications rather than assuming all are entry level.

An ABPI case study describes an individual entering regulatory affairs following a pharmacy degree and hospital foundation training, illustrating that a dedicated industry rotation is not the only route. University careers guidance likewise describes both graduate schemes and direct applications to junior roles. If you require visa sponsorship, check the specific future employer and role; the sponsorship status of a foundation programme does not determine a later industry's hiring decision.

14How do I turn the experience into strong applications and interviews?

Instead of writing “completed a pharmaceutical industry placement”, make your contribution specific. A stronger illustrative CV bullet would read: “Reviewed published evidence for a supervised medical-information exercise and produced a referenced summary used in an internal learning session.” Only include deliverables you genuinely completed, and obtain permission before describing work involving confidential projects.

Build two or three interview stories using Situation → Task → Action → Result → Reflection. Potential themes are responding to critical feedback, summarising conflicting evidence, communicating with someone from another function, following a quality process or balancing project work with clinical training. Results need not be invented percentages: an accepted document, a clear presentation or a documented improvement can be meaningful if true.

Keep an “industry role map” with three functions that interest you, the key skills each needs, relevant vacancies and people who can explain the work. Ask your supervisor whether they can provide a reference and whether you may stay in touch. Apply as roles open; don't wait until the end of the year to learn when graduate programmes recruit.

Sources and further reading: University of Edinburgh — life sciences careers and entry routes · ABPI — real job case studies · ABPI — regulatory affairs

15Will splitting the year disadvantage my registration assessment or learning outcomes?

There is no sound basis for promising either that a split guarantees better assessment performance or that it puts every trainee at a disadvantage. What matters is the quality and coverage of the approved training plan, supervised patient-facing learning, assessment preparation and your own consistent study. Industry-specific activities may support professional judgement, governance, evidence appraisal and communication, while clinical rotations help develop prescribing and direct patient-care competencies.

Ask for the learning-outcome mapping across all three settings, who reviews your e-portfolio evidence, when progress reviews occur, how prescribing learning is supported, and what happens if a planned experience is unavailable. NHS England explains that a national assessment strategy and e-portfolio support trainees regardless of sector. Employers are expected to provide an approved training plan and appropriate designated supervision, including arrangements during multi-sector rotations.

Use your foundation provider and regulator for current assessment rules and deadlines. The title of a rotation does not override those requirements; neither does an anecdote from a trainee on Reddit establish your likely result.

Sources and further reading: NHS England — foundation training guidance · NHS England — 2026 employer training requirements · GPhC — official pharmacy regulator

16What should I ask the placement coordinator now that I have an offer?

Request answers in writing where possible. The key questions are: (1) Which company, department and team will host me? (2) How many weeks will I spend in each setting, and in what order? (3) What are the exact sites, working hours, travel expectations and any expense arrangements? (4) Who is my lead designated supervisor, and who supports me at each rotation site? (5) What are the expected industry projects and assessment evidence? (6) Are medical affairs, regulatory, safety or clinical trials visits genuinely scheduled, or just potential? (7) Can I meet previous trainees or attend introductory sessions? (8) How will registration-assessment preparation and supervised clinical practice be organised?

Also ask whether the first months of the placement can include an agreed small project, which types of team meetings you may attend, whether shadowing requires special approval and how to report confidentiality or safety concerns. Those are constructive questions for an enthusiastic new trainee, not unreasonable demands. The aim is to turn a vague programme title into a concrete training and career-development plan.
